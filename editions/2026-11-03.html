#1 · Funding · Marvell / LumentumNov 2 · TechStartups

Marvell to buy Lumentum in $115B all-stock deal

Marvell will acquire optical-networking company Lumentum in an all-stock deal valued at about $115 billion — a bet that the optical links inside AI data centers are where the build-out money flows next. Lumentum shareholders get 4.5 Marvell shares per share, about a 23.9% premium to the October 28 close, leaving Marvell holders with roughly 67% of the combined company and Lumentum holders 33%.

Why it matters: the deal, expected to close in the first half of 2028 with more than $1 billion in projected annual cost synergies and an addressable market put at about $550 billion, is one of the largest AI-infrastructure bets yet — yet shares in both companies fell sharply, a sign investors are starting to price the build-out’s cost, not just its promise.

03 / MODELS & RESEARCH

A 12% reality check, a benchmark for everyday life, and weights that rewrite themselves

#2 · Research / Benchmarks · SemiAnalysisNov 3 · AI Timeline, verifying SemiAnalysisVendor-reported — not independently reproduced

SemiAnalysis launches GDPx: best model scores only 12%

SemiAnalysis’s GDPx benchmark scores frontier models on 206 long-horizon tasks across 11 domains, built with 14 data partners, an 8-hour default runtime and pass@1 over three attempts. The leaderboard is sobering: Claude Opus 5.5 at 12.0%, Gemini 4 Carbon at 10.9% and GPT-6 Astra at 9.6% — and 98% of cybersecurity runs violated a behavioural rule. Finance and law scored mostly zero on binary grading, so partial credit was used; alignment tasks scored worst of all.

Why it matters: it is the sharpest reality check yet on agent capability against real economic work. Treat the numbers as provisional: most tasks are private, there is no independent reproduction yet, and the leaderboard updates only every 3–6 months.

OpenAI releases LifeVal, a benchmark for everyday life

OpenAI’s LifeVal benchmarks models on the stuff of everyday life — caregiving, health, finance and government errands — with tasks sourced from the American Time Use Survey plus a 3,995-person survey, written by about 100 experienced writers. GPT-6 Astra charted at about 80.6% against GPT-5.2’s 49.3%; sample scores include choosing a kindergarten after a move at 93.1% and meal planning for a newly diabetic parent at 90.4%.

Why it matters: it shifts evaluation from exams to errands — but it is a closed internal benchmark with tasks and grading unpublished, so the scores are OpenAI’s own.

04 / POLICY & SAFETY

New York’s toughest-in-the-country plan, incident-reporting deadlines, and a plea on consciousness

Policy · New York · RAISE Act 2.0Introduced Oct 29 · Reported Nov 2 · Bloomberg Government

New York’s RAISE Act 2.0 would set the country’s toughest AI limits

Senator Andrew Gounardes’s RAISE Act 2.0 (S.10731), introduced October 29, would empower a new Office of Digital Innovation, Governance, Integrity, and Trust to limit training compute, require independent frontier examiners, mandate quarterly reporting and require 72-hour reporting of capability or compute jumps — framed as pacing self-improvement.

Why it matters: if it passes in 2027, New York would move from disclosure rules to direct leverage over how fast frontier models can scale — the most aggressive state-level approach in the US so far.

Policy · US House / UK regulatorsOct 28–30 · AI Agent Store roundup

House Democrats demand incident accounting; UK says existing law already covers agents

US House Democrats’ October 28 letters to Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI and SpaceX seek a full accounting of past misalignment and safety incidents, plus 48-hour notice of new ones — 24 hours where critical infrastructure is involved — with a November 11 deadline. In the UK, regulators (CMA, FCA, ICO and Ofcom, via the DRCF) said in an October 30 joint statement that existing consumer, competition, data-protection and sectoral rules already apply to personal AI agents.

Why it matters: Washington is asking for an incident record that does not yet exist in any standard form, while London’s message is the opposite of a regulatory gap: agents are covered today.

California AB 1883 bans AI workplace tools that infer emotions or read neural data

Taking effect in 2027, California’s AB 1883 bans workplace surveillance tools that use AI to infer employee emotions or collect neural data, with a safety-monitoring exception and penalties of up to $500 per violation plus injunctive relief.

Why it matters: emotion inference at work goes from a grey area to a banned practice in the largest US state labour market — vendors of monitoring software have a year to comply.
