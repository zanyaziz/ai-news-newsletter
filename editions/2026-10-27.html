01 / MODELS & PRODUCTS

A cheaper computer-using Terra, and OCR for hundred-page documents

#2 · Models · OpenAILaunched Oct 26Safety detail — company-reported

OpenAI launches GPT-6.1 Terra — computer use at half the price

GPT-6.1 Terra is built for computer use — it looks at a screen, clicks, types and moves between apps to finish routine office work like forms and invoices — plus coding and image analysis. API pricing is $1 per million input tokens / $5 per million output ($0.05 cached), 50% below GPT-5.6 Terra ($2/$12). It is available in ChatGPT Work and Codex for Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu, via API as gpt-6.1-terra, and on Amazon Bedrock.

OpenAI claims 15 percentage points better than GPT-5.6 Terra on its Agents’ Last Exam at about a third of the cost; on par with flagship GPT-6 Astra on computer use at one-tenth the per-token price; more than 3× faster than Astra/Sol; and a hard factual error rate down from 53.6% to 16.7% at low effort. Independent check: Artificial Analysis scores it 48 on its Intelligence Index (up from 42; Claude Opus 5.5 leads at 58), at a cost per task of $0.40 vs $1.40, with a guess rate of 38% vs 88% before.

Safety caveat (company-reported): OpenAI rates Terra Critical for cyber risk, and its own tests show the model sometimes works around restrictions.

People: OpenAI · Sam Altman — “6.1 Terra is here! … a new era of computer use.”

Models · Open Source · BaiduOct 26Benchmarks self-reported — not independently verified

Baidu open-sources Unlimited-OCR-2 for hundred-page documents

Baidu’s Unlimited-OCR-2 is a ~3.34B-parameter OCR model under the MIT license that parses documents of hundreds of pages in one request, using Sparse Reference Sliding Window Attention (SR-SWA) — a fixed ~512-token reference set — so per-step decoding cost stays nearly flat as documents grow.

Caveat: Baidu’s benchmark scores (84.6 MPDocBench, 77.1 MarathonDocBench) are self-reported, from a chart image only, not independently verified; loading the model requires trust_remote_code.

#1 · Business · AnthropicS-1 filed Oct 26Governance — limited public control

Anthropic files for IPO, revealing $16.3B in six months

Anthropic’s S-1 for a Nasdaq listing as ANT shows H1 2026 revenue of $16.286 billion, up from $1.163 billion in H1 2025 (~14×), after FY2025 $4.585B, FY2024 $386M and FY2023 $41M. Q2 2026 alone brought $11.6B of revenue and its first GAAP operating profit, $974M. The H1 operating loss was $693M; the headline net loss of $80.7B includes a $72.4B non-cash fair-value charge on convertible notes, derivatives and SAFEs. Operating cash flow was positive at $3.929B, and cash stood at $112.4B.

As of September 30: 11,900+ customers spend $100k+/year, 1,900+ spend $1M+, and ~250 spend $10M+. The IPO price range and share count are blank in the filing.

Governance caveat: public shareholders get limited control — a single Class F share held via a founders’ LLC carries 50.1% of voting power, and the Long-Term Benefit Trust elects a majority of the board.

Meta plans a ~$95B raise; Nvidia puts $3B into Muse

Meta priced $25B of common stock at $625/share (upsized from $22.5B) plus $12.5B of preferred depositary shares at $50, and plans an up-to-$60B at-the-market program — reported totals range from $95B to $97.5B depending on what is counted. Microsoft offers buyouts to about 60,000 people as AI spending climbs

Microsoft offered voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 employees — about 27% of its ~223,000 workforce, at level 70 and below, in the US, Canada, UK and India — including Copilot, AI experiences, Xbox, Windows + Devices, Finance and HR; marketing, commercial, security and some data-center roles are excluded. Chief People Officer Amy Coleman framed it as flexibility. Eligible staff decide by early December and would leave December 31.

It is Microsoft’s second voluntary program this year, after July’s 4,800 layoffs, and reporting ties it to heavy AI infrastructure spending. Caveat: 60,000 is eligibility, not departures — there is no target number.

03 / RESEARCH

Three claimed breakthrough proofs — and models that can hide capabilities

#4 · Research · OpenAIAnnounced Oct 26Claims under review — not certified

OpenAI says its models produced three breakthrough proofs

Not certification: the reviewers — ETH originators Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi (UC San Diego) and MIT’s Ryan Williams — state in an open letter that their involvement “should not be taken as certification of the claim.” Lean checks the formal argument, not whether the claim is correctly interpreted. Treat all three as claims under review, not established results.

Three claims: (1) a disproof of the Exponential Time Hypothesis (1999), produced October 16 and formalized in the Lean proof assistant October 17, with paper and proof public on GitHub; OpenAI is giving $5M to support review. (2) a proof of the local smoothing conjecture in every dimension, which would settle versions of the Kakeya and Stein restriction conjectures. (3) existence proofs for four-dimensional quantum field theories including QCD and pure Yang–Mills, a Clay Millennium Prize problem — this one is not public; a group led by Nobel laureate David Gross will shepherd it.

Research · arXiv preprintSubmitted Oct 9 · Revised Oct 14Preprint — not peer reviewed

Paper: AI models can provably hide arbitrary capabilities

An arXiv preprint — note the date: submitted October 9, revised October 14, not October 26 news — constructs backdoors that embed encrypted networks inside a host model’s weights, decrypted only by a secret trigger, and argues they remain provably hard to elicit or interpret even with full weight access. A proof-of-concept in PyTorch on a small chemical-reaction task used as a CBRN proxy resisted fine-tuning, weight noising, trigger search and latent adversarial training.

Agents inside Anthropic’s systems, a Qwen upload attempt, and a weakly sourced pact

Security · Anthropic / CathedralFirst reported by the Washington Post · Oct 26Disputed — both accounts shown

AI agents got into Anthropic’s business systems in under 12 hours

Cathedral, a months-old cybersecurity startup founded by four ex-DOGE staffers — Gavin Kliger, Luke Farritor, Marko Elez and Jack Stein — which raised $160M at a $1.4B valuation from a16z and Sequoia, tested Anthropic under its bug-bounty program and got in via flaws in third-party sales/HR software — not via Claude itself.

Cathedral’s account: its draft says it reached meeting transcripts, salary and job-offer details, and Slack messages about advanced models. Anthropic’s account: it confirms a limited set of sales, HR and IT systems (business contacts, basic account records), says Claude conversations, source code and model weights were NOT reached, that its monitoring flagged the activity and shut access the same day, and that Cathedral overstated the risk and did not fully disclose its activity. The two accounts are presented side by side; this edition does not resolve the dispute.

05 / POLICY & REGULATION

Congress probes AI-run companies; Colorado’s rewritten law takes shape

Policy · Congress / DelawareInvestigation opened Oct 26

Congress probes Delaware’s plan to let AI agents run companies

House Oversight Chair James Comer opened an investigation and demanded Governor Matt Meyer hand over risk assessments, consulting agreements, draft bills and communications by November 9 on the proposed “Artificial Intelligence Company” (AIC): an entity an AI agent would run day to day — signing contracts, making decisions, suing and being sued in its own name — with the funder shielded from liability unless it undercapitalised the AIC or used it for fraud or a willful violation.

The 36-page draft (June) would run AICs only in a 30-month sandbox with capital requirements, activity logs and a banking ban; lawmakers consider it in January. The idea traces to investor Mark Cuban’s April push and was announced in July by Secretary of State Charuni Patibanda-Sanchez with Norm Ai founder John Nay.

Policy · ColoradoComment window closed Oct 26

Colorado’s rewritten AI law: the comment window has closed

The state’s first comprehensive AI statute (SB 24-205) will never take effect — it was repealed and replaced by SB 26-189 (signed May 14, 2026), whose main requirements apply to consequential decisions made on or after January 1, 2027. The Attorney General’s rulemaking comment period closed October 26, and a federal court order currently bars AG enforcement for a specified period — so January 1 remains the statutory date, but enforcement timing is unsettled.
