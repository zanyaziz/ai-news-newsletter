02 / RESEARCH & BREAKTHROUGHS

Proofs, priority, and the genome

OpenAISept. 8Widely reported

OpenAI claims its AI solved the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize problem

OpenAI says roughly 10,000 concurrent AI agents, using an unreleased model more capable than GPT-6 Astra and Lean formal verification, produced a proof that the forced Navier–Stokes equations can develop finite-time “blowup.” Nature and The Wall Street Journal treated it as a landmark AI-for-math result.

The 165-page proof still needs independent verification, mathematicians caution. OpenAI says it will not claim the $1 million Millennium Prize.

Why it matters

If verified, this would be a major mathematical result and a striking demonstration of coordinated AI agents doing frontier research.

Priority dispute erupts: NYU mathematician says OpenAI rushed his unpublished work

Hours before OpenAI’s announcement, NYU mathematician Tristan Buckmaster—working with Anthropic’s Levent Alpöge—said OpenAI had learned of their related Euler-equations proof and rushed its own announcement. Buckmaster questioned whether private Codex sessions had been accessed.

OpenAI’s Sébastien Bubeck and CEO Sam Altman denied the claim, although OpenAI said it “cannot rule out” de-identified user data helping train its models. The dispute was covered by multiple outlets, but the underlying claims rest on statements and posts from the parties.

Google DeepMindSept. 8Widely reported

Google DeepMind launches AlphaGenome Atlas: predicted effects of all 9 billion human DNA variants

DeepMind released a one-petabyte, no-code-searchable database of AlphaGenome predictions for every possible single-letter DNA change—about 9 billion variants. A new Variant Impact score found causal variants at 29.5%, versus 12.5% for CADD, and the atlas also includes more than 100 million indels and over 2,500 regulatory motifs.

Developed with the Stowers Institute and academic partners, the work is currently a bioRxiv preprint and is free for non-commercial use.

03 / POLICY & SAFETY

Warnings leave the lab

AnthropicSept. 8–9Widely reported

Anthropic researcher Jacob Coxon resigns Sept. 8 warning AI could “kill us all”; Congress reacts

Jacob Coxon resigned in a viral Sept. 8 post, saying frontier labs are “racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives,” and claiming many builders believe AI could “kill us all by the end of the decade.” Senior Anthropic researcher Evan Hubinger replied that Coxon was correct and estimated a less-than-10% chance over the next decade.

On Sept. 9, bipartisan lawmakers demanded emergency hearings and floated slowdown legislation. OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki had echoed caution in a Sept. 6 blog post.
